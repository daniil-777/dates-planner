§0

The answer, before the detail

Three questions were asked — what custom ML, can images be generated locally, can Apple's tooling be used. The short answers are a great deal, yes but off the request path, and yes, in exactly two of your four runtimes.

The most valuable machine learning in this app is not another LLM call. It is a handful of small models trained on your own ledger, shipped the way the two-head classifier already is: fitted in Python, exported as JSON weights, executed in TypeScript, held to a parity test. That pattern is the single best thing in this repository, and almost every idea below is a re-use of it.

The second most valuable thing is moving inference off the server and onto the device. Today a photograph of your face travels to a datacentre so a frontier model can say "content, 0.78". A 52-dimension face-landmark vector computed in the browser, fed to a logistic head you trained on your own taps, gives the same answer in 20 milliseconds, for nothing, offline, with the photograph never leaving the phone. That is a better product and a better privacy story at the same time.

Apple's frameworks — Core ML, Vision, Foundation Models, Speech, Image Playground — are genuinely excellent and genuinely unreachable from where your code runs today. They are native-process APIs. Your server is Linux on Fly.io; your client is a web page. They become available the moment you add either a Mac-side sidecar (for training and batch work) or a thin native shell around the PWA (for on-device features your users actually get). Both are described below, with the boundary drawn precisely.

If you only do three things

Ranked by value delivered per hour spent, given the code that already exists.

  • First

    Mood without the cloud

    Face landmarks in the browser → a 5-class logistic head trained on your own mood taps. Kills a paid API call, works on a plane, and the photo never leaves the device. Same export-and-parity pattern as the classifier. ~2 days.

  • Second

    Find the trips nobody logged

    Cluster confirmed expenses in time and space, propose an Event when a cluster is far from home. No training, no labels, uses lat/lon you already store. Turns a ledger into a memory. ~1 day.

  • Third

    Postcards from the ledger

    A nightly job on your Mac turns each memory's facts into a FLUX prompt and renders an illustration for the timeline. Never on the request path, never blocking a user. 30–90 s per image on an M3 Pro. ~2 days.

What you already have

Six pieces of machine learning are already in this repository, and one of them — the training loop that closes itself — is worth more than anything proposed below, because every new model can borrow it.

Two-head classifier srv/lib/classifier crc32-hashed char n-grams (2–4, word-bounded, 65 536 buckets, L2) + 7 dense clock features → two multinomial logistic heads. 4.9 MB of JSON weights, scored in TypeScript, byte-checked against Python. The constraint that governs every decision below: your dataset is tiny

A two-person household produces on the order of 150–500 confirmed expenses a year, a few dozen events, a few hundred photos, maybe a thousand chat messages. That is three to four orders of magnitude short of what a deep network needs to be trained from scratch, and it is the reason the existing classifier is a linear model over hashed n-grams rather than a transformer — a good decision that should be repeated.

The consequences are concrete. Train small: logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, k-NN over embeddings. Borrow representations rather than learning them: take embeddings from a pretrained encoder (CLIP for photos, MiniLM for text) and train only a small head on top. Prefer geometry to learning where geometry suffices — trip detection is clustering, recurrence detection is autocorrelation, neither needs a label. And personalise as a residual: one shared base model plus a per-household correction, never one model per household trained from nothing.

§2

Four runtimes, and what each one can hold

Every proposal in this document has to answer one question first: where does the arithmetic happen? There are exactly four answers, they have very different capabilities, and the difference between them is what decides whether Apple's frameworks are available at all.

Lane B

The web page

The most underrated runtime you own. Safari 26 ships WebGPU on by default across macOS, iOS and iPadOS, which means ONNX Runtime Web and transformers.js get real GPU acceleration on a phone. MediaPipe's face landmarker runs at 30 fps on a mid-range device with no GPU at all.

Its superpower is jurisdictional, not technical: data processed here never becomes data you hold. A face never uploaded is a face you never have to protect, retain, or delete.

Good for
camera pre-flight, face/gesture, small embeddings, sentiment, offline inference
Bad for
anything over ~200 MB of weights; first-load cost is real
Cost
zero, forever
Lane A

The CAP server

Where the ledger lives, so it is where anything needing all the data belongs: clustering across a year of expenses, recurrence detection, forecasting, the statement. It already scores a 4.9 MB model in about a millisecond.

Its limit is the operating system. Fly.io runs linux/amd64. There is no Neural Engine and there never will be a Core ML call here. If you want an ONNX model on the server, onnxruntime-node is the honest answer.

Good for
whole-ledger models, batch jobs, anything needing secrets
Bad for
heavy GPU work; the Fly machine is small and shared
Cost
your existing bill, plus RAM for anything you load
Lane C

Your Mac

36 GB of unified memory on an M3 Pro is a serious ML workstation. It can fine-tune a small model, render FLUX images, run a 30B-parameter LLM in 4-bit, and convert anything to Core ML. It already trains your classifier.

The rule that keeps this sane: nothing here is ever on the request path. Its deliverables are committed artefacts — weights.json, a rendered PNG, a new fixture — reviewed by you and deployed like code.

Good for
training, image generation, transcription backfills, experiments
Bad for
anything a user waits on; the laptop is closed half the time
Cost
electricity and patience
Lane D

A native shell

A SwiftUI app whose entire body is a WKWebView pointed at your PWA, plus a message handler that exposes native capabilities to JavaScript. Perhaps 300 lines. In exchange you get Vision, Core ML on the Neural Engine, the on-device Foundation Models LLM, SpeechAnalyzer, Image Playground, and Siri.

The price is a real one: an Apple Developer account, code signing, TestFlight, and a second deployment path forever. Worth it only once two or three lane-D features are on the list — which, by §4, they are.

Good for
OCR, faces, on-device LLM, dictation, generated art, widgets
Bad for
anyone on Android or a laptop — build it as an enhancement, never a requirement
Cost
USD 99/year and a release process

The Apple toolbox, exactly

You asked what Apple's ML tooling can actually do for this app. Here is the whole shelf, with the honest reachability column — because half of these are magnificent and unreachable until you build the wrapper in §3.3.

§3.1 — every Apple ML framework worth your time, and where it lands

Core ML + coremltools D C Runs a converted model (.mlpackage) on CPU, GPU or the Neural Engine, picking automatically. Converts from PyTorch, TensorFlow, scikit-learn and ONNX. A fifth LlmProvider. Memory titles, chat reply suggestions, a first draft of the yearly statement — all with no key, no bill, no network. 4 096 tokens of context, shared between prompt and answer. It has no world knowledge and it is not Opus. Great at rewriting your data; poor at anything requiring facts.
Speech — SpeechAnalyzer + SpeechTranscriber D The transcription engine behind Notes and Voice Memos, exposed since the 26 SDKs. Long-form, on-device only, streamed as an AsyncSequence. Your chat voice notes, transcribed on the sender's phone before upload. Searchable, quotable, accessible — and it makes voice notes indexable by the semantic search in §4.6. On-device only by design, so it cannot run server-side to backfill the archive. Model assets download on first use.
Natural Language D C Sentence embeddings, contextual embeddings, sentiment scoring (−1…+1), tokenisation, language identification, named entities — all offline, all tiny. Mood-from-text on chat messages and memory notes. Language ID to pick between your EN/DE/RU strings automatically. Embeddings are weaker than a modern MiniLM. Fine for sentiment, second-best for search.
Translation D On-device translation between downloaded language pairs, with a plain Swift API. The app already speaks English, German and Russian. A household that mixes languages in chat could read every message in its own. Language packs are a user-visible download. Native only.
Image Playground / Genmoji D Apple Intelligence image generation as a framework: build an ImagePlaygroundConcept from text (or a PencilKit drawing), get an image back — on-device where possible, in Animation, Illustration and Sketch styles plus the newer ChatGPT-backed ones. A generated sticker per person. An illustration for a memory, made on the phone, in seconds, for free. Style-limited by design — you will not get a photoreal postcard out of it. It refuses photorealistic people on purpose. That is exactly why §7 keeps FLUX on the Mac as well.
MLX / mlx-swift C D Apple's array framework built for unified memory — NumPy-shaped Python API, faster than PyTorch-MPS for the same model. LoRA fine-tuning, quantisation, LLM serving. Strictly lane C.
MFLUX / DiffusionKit / Draw Things C MLX-native image generation. FLUX.1-schnell renders 1024×1024 in roughly 30 s on an M3 Pro at 4-bit; the bigger FLUX models take around 90 s. Every generated illustration in §7. Local, free, unlimited, no content pipeline to explain to anybody. Minutes, not milliseconds. Never put this in an HTTP handler.
App Intents, Widgets, Live Activities D Not ML, but the reason lane D pays for itself: "Hey Siri, log a coffee for eleven eighty", a home-screen widget with this month's total, a Live Activity during a trip. The next-action prediction of §4.5 has somewhere to go — a widget is the natural surface for a single predicted next action. Ties you to iOS. Design them as garnish, never as the only path to a feature.
§3.2

Reaching lane C: the sidecar you already designed

CLASSIFIER_URL is the pattern. Any HTTP endpoint that speaks the contract replaces local inference and falls back silently when it is gone. A Swift daemon on your Mac exposing Vision and Foundation Models over localhost plugs into the same socket, with the same failure behaviour. 

# ml/serve.py already proves the shape. A Swift twin:
APPLE_VISION_URL=http://127.0.0.1:8090/ocr
APPLE_LLM_URL=http://127.0.0.1:8090/generate

# Only ever set in .env.local — never in fly.toml, because
# the Fly machine cannot reach your laptop and should not try.

Useful for development and backfills: re-OCR every stored receipt with Vision overnight and compare it against what Document AI returned, without spending a franc. Not useful in production, and the fallback path means that is safe.

§3.3

Reaching lane D: about 300 lines of Swift

A single-view app, a WKWebView filling it, and a message handler that answers JavaScript. The web app keeps every feature it has; native capabilities appear as an optional object it can feature-detect. 

// Swift side — one handler, one switch.
func userContentController(_ c: WKUserContentController,
     didReceive m: WKScriptMessage) {
  switch m.name {
  case "ocr":       // VNRecognizeTextRequest
  case "faceMood":  // Vision landmarks → your Core ML head
  case "transcribe":// SpeechAnalyzer
  case "generate":  // SystemLanguageModel session
  default: break
  }
}
// Web side — degrade to what you already do.
const native = window.webkit?.messageHandlers
const reading = native
  ? await bridge('faceMood', frame)   // on the ANE
  : await localHead(landmarks)        // lane B fallback

The rule that keeps lane D from becoming a trap

Every native capability is a faster path to a result the web app can already produce, never the only path to it. Vision OCR speeds up a scan that Document AI would have handled anyway. The Foundation Models draft is a free version of what an Anthropic call writes better. If a feature cannot degrade gracefully to lane A or B, it does not get built — otherwise half your household is locked out by the phone they own.

R · Reading — making the scan smarter before the server sees it

Today the phone is a dumb camera: it resizes a photo and posts it. Everything that goes wrong — a blurred frame, a glare stripe across the total, a photo of a menu instead of a receipt — costs a full round trip, a Document AI call, and a human's patience. Almost all of that can be caught in thirty milliseconds on the device, and one very common Swiss case can skip the cloud entirely.

R1

Capture coach

B

Variance of the Laplacian for sharpness, a clipped-highlight ratio for glare, and a coverage estimate from a Sobel edge map — three numbers, computed on a downscaled canvas, updated live while the camera is open. The shutter goes green when all three pass.

No model, no training, no dependency. Roughly 80 lines beside useCamera.ts, and it removes the most common failure in the whole app.

none — classical image statistics

A dewarped, cropped receipt improves every downstream step: OCR accuracy, file size, and the confidence the classifier reports.

R3

Swiss QR-bill decoder

B

Not machine learning at all, and probably the highest-value item in this family. Every Swiss invoice since 2020 carries a QR block with a Swiss cross in the middle encoding creditor, IBAN, exact amount, currency and reference in a fixed 31-line format. Safari has a native BarcodeDetector; the payload is a spec, not a guess.

Result: a perfectly-populated draft with confidence 1.0, produced offline, with no extraction cost and no ambiguity. Restaurant receipts stay on the model path; invoices stop needing one.

C · Classifying — six more heads on the model you already have

The feature vector is built once per transaction and then thrown at two logistic heads. Adding a head costs a label column in the training CSV and about forty lines of Python. Everything in this family reuses features.py unchanged, which means the parity test protects it for free.

C1

Honest confidence

CA

A logistic regression trained with C=12 on L2-normalised features is over-confident — it will say 0.97 and be wrong. Fit a one-parameter temperature on a held-out split, store it beside the weights, divide the logits by it before the softmax. Twenty lines, and suddenly 0.7 means seven times out of ten.

Then use it. Below a calibrated threshold the draft shows no guess at all rather than a bad one — needsReview already exists to carry exactly that.

Model
temperature scaling per head
Data
the existing held-out split
Effort
hours
C2

Ask about the ones it doesn't know

A

Active learning, and it is nearly free. Rank unconfirmed drafts by the entropy of the predicted distribution and surface the most uncertain one first. A household confirming ten receipts a week teaches the model far more if those ten are the confusing ones.

Every answer lands in Corrections, which is already the training set. The loop closes itself.

Model
none — a sort key over existing probabilities
Data

C3

One model, personalised per household

C

Multi-tenancy arrived in the model with TWM-ADR-002. Each group's ledger is far too small to train on alone, and a shared model gets one household's Coop habits wrong. The answer is a residual: keep the shared base weights, and learn a small per-group correction from that group's own Corrections rows only.

Concretely, a second sparse weight matrix over the same 65 536 buckets, fitted with heavy regularisation on 30–80 rows, added to the base logits before the softmax. It serialises the same way, loads the same way, and falls back to the base when a group is new.

Model
base logits + per-group residual, same feature space

Merchant embeddings

C

It also gives you a better duplicate test than string distance: two spellings of the same shop end up in the same place.

C6

Which event does this belong to?

A

A learned version can come later from the accept/reject signal. Start with the three features and a hand-set weight — it will be right most of the time.

E · Structuring — finding the story already in the ledger

This family needs no labels, no training and no GPU. It is geometry and signal processing over rows you already have, and it produces the single most emotionally valuable output in the app: we went somewhere, and nobody wrote it down, and here it is.

E1

Trips found by clustering

A

DBSCAN over (date, lat, lon) with a haversine-plus-days metric, run nightly over confirmed expenses that have no event. Home is the median of the everyday band, so it needs no configuration. A cluster of three or more postings whose centroid is far from home, spanning one to fourteen days, is a candidate trip.

Never auto-create. Propose it on the timeline with the dates, the total, and the places, and let a human name it. A wrongly-invented trip is much worse than a missed one.

Output is two useful things: a forecast of what the month already owes, and a gentle "you have been paying for this since 2024".

M · Mood — the same feature, without sending a face anywhere

srv/lib/mood.ts already gets two hard things right: the photograph is never stored, and the reading is presented as an estimate to confirm rather than a fact. What it gets wrong is placement — the photograph still travels to a datacentre, which makes the feature cost money, require a network, and put an image of a face in a request body. All three go away if the model runs in the page.

M1

Mood from blendshapes

B

MediaPipe's face landmarker returns 478 3-D landmarks plus 52 named blendshape scores — mouthSmileLeft, browDownRight, eyeSquintLeft — each 0…1, computed in the browser at video rate. That is a 52-dimension feature vector describing an expression, with the image already thrown away.

Train a five-class logistic regression on it, using your own Moods rows as labels: every time somebody taps a face on the mood page with the camera open, you have a labelled example. Two hundred examples is plenty for a linear model over 52 clean features.

Ship it exactly like the classifier: fit in ml/, export weights.json, port the scorer to TypeScript, add a parity fixture. A 52×5 matrix is 40 KB.

Model
multinomial logistic over 52 blendshapes
Data
your Moods taps, captured with consent
Effort
~2 days
M2

BD

Use it to prompt, never to record. "Sounds like a good evening — want to save that as a 4?" is welcome. Silently writing a mood row because somebody typed a sad sentence is surveillance.

Two rules this family inherits from the code that already exists

The photograph is never stored — that is already the documented behaviour of detectMood, and moving inference into the browser strengthens it rather than replacing it. And a reading is a suggestion until a human taps it: the model fills the picker, the person confirms, and source records which happened. Neither rule is negotiable, and both are what make this feature acceptable at all — see §9.

P · Anticipating — suggestion, and the next thing you were going to do

"Predict the next action" sounds like it needs a sequence model. It does not. A household has perhaps eight things it ever does in this app, and which one comes next is decided almost entirely by the clock, the calendar and what is left undone. That makes it a ranking problem over a handful of candidate actions with a dozen features — a gradient-boosted tree on a few hundred rows, or an honest set of rules until you have enough of them.

S · Remembering — making four years of rows findable

The app's whole promise is that it keeps a timeline of memories. A timeline you can only scroll is a worse archive than a shoebox. Embeddings fix this completely, and at this data volume the index is a few hundred kilobytes.

The loop every one of these ships through

You already built the hard part. Any new model — mood, payer, personalisation — enters the app through the same seven steps, and the parity test is the gate that makes the whole thing trustworthy rather than merely clever.

One bug to fix before any of this

§6

Three recipes you could start tonight

Concrete enough to follow. Each one names the files it creates, the commands it runs, and the test that proves it works.

6.1 · The on-device mood model, end to end

The flagship recommendation, built entirely inside patterns this repository already has. Nothing here is novel; it is the classifier's pipeline pointed at a different vector.

  1. Collect, with consent and in the open. On the mood page, when the camera is open and somebody taps a face, also record the 52 blendshape values for that frame. Store them in a new MoodSamples table with the level they chose. Say plainly in the UI that this is teaching the app, and give it an off switch. Two weeks of ordinary use is enough.
  2. Export. A sibling to scripts/export-training-data.ts that writes ml/data/mood_samples.csv — 52 float columns plus level.
  3. Fit. ml/train_mood.py: standardise the 52 columns, fit a multinomial logistic regression, print a confusion matrix and a five-fold accuracy. Expect roughly 60–75% exact-level accuracy and near-95% within one level, which is plenty for a picker that pre-selects a face.
  4. Export weights. ml/export_mood.py writes ml/model/mood_weights.json (means, scales, coefficients, intercepts, label order) and a parity fixture of 200 rows.
  5. Port and gate. app/src/pages/mood/moodModel.ts — standardise, multiply, softmax, argmax. Forty lines. Then test/mood-parity.test.ts asserting agreement to 1e-4, exactly like classifier-parity.test.ts.
  6. Wire it up. The camera path calls the local model first and only falls back to detectMood when the local confidence is below threshold and a key exists. The photo never leaves the browser on the local path — which is now the default path. 
# the whole loop, once the two Python files exist
npm run mood:export-data          # → ml/data/mood_samples.csv
ml/.venv/bin/python ml/train_mood.py
ml/.venv/bin/python ml/export_mood.py
npx vitest run test/mood-parity.test.ts

# the browser side, loaded once and cached by the service worker
import { FaceLandmarker, FilesetResolver } from '@mediapipe/tasks-vision'
const lm = await FaceLandmarker.createFromOptions(files, {
  baseOptions: { modelAssetPath: '/models/face_landmarker.task' },
  outputFaceBlendshapes: true, runningMode: 'IMAGE',
})
const shapes = lm.detect(frame).faceBlendshapes[0].categories  // 52 scores
const reading = scoreMood(shapes)   // your weights.json, ~20 ms

Why this is the right first project. It removes a recurring cost, it works offline, it strengthens a privacy stance the code already takes, it needs no new runtime and no native app, and it produces a second worked example of the train-in-Python-score-in-TypeScript pattern — which makes every later model cheaper.

6.2 · Trips, with no training at all

Sixty lines in srv/lib/trips.ts, a nightly node-cron job you already depend on, and a card on the timeline. The only judgement calls are three parameters, and all three have defensible defaults. 

# the metric: a day is worth about 50 km, so a three-day
# cluster 150 km away is as "tight" as a same-day one 10 km away
d(a, b) = haversine(a.latlon, b.latlon) + 50 * |a.day - b.day|

eps         = 120 km      # neighbourhood radius in that metric
minPoints   = 3           # fewer is a day out, not a trip
homeRadius  = 15 km       # median of all postings = home

# keep a cluster when: |cluster| >= minPoints
#                 and distance(centroid, home) > homeRadius
#                 and 1 <= span(days) <= 14
#                 and no expense in it already has an event

Evaluate it on your own ledger before shipping. Run it over the last two years and read the output yourself: every cluster it proposes should be a trip you remember, and every trip you remember should appear. That is a better test than any metric, and it takes ten minutes.

Two failure modes to design against. A long stay abroad becomes one enormous cluster — cap the span. A commute to another city looks like a weekly trip — exclude clusters whose merchants repeat monthly, which the recurrence detector in E2 already identifies.

6.3 · Personalising without a model per household

The tenancy work in TWM-ADR-002 makes this both possible and necessary. The shape below keeps one shared model and adds a small, heavily-regularised correction per group — which is the only thing that works when a group has forty labelled rows. 

# train.py, unchanged, produces the base:  W_base  (65543 × K)
# then, per group, on that group's Corrections only:

X_g, y_g   = features(group_rows), labels(group_rows)
base_logit = X_g @ W_base                 # frozen, never refitted
residual   = LogisticRegression(C=0.05,       # strong prior toward zero
                               warm_start=False)
residual.fit(X_g, y_g, sample_weight=w)   # w down-weights old corrections

# serving:  softmax(base_logit + λ · residual_logit),  λ = min(1, n_g / 50)
# a brand-new group gets λ = 0 and behaves exactly as today

The λ ramp is the safety property. A household with three corrections cannot move the model; a household with fifty owns its own vocabulary. And because the residual lives in the same 65 536-bucket feature space, it serialises, loads and scores through code that already exists — loadModel gains a second matrix, nothing else changes.

Contract impact: this is a change to docs/CONTRACTS.md §3 and §5 — a new optional block in weights.json and a new field in ClassifyResult naming which residual answered. Change the document first, then the Python, then the TypeScript, then the fixture. That order is what keeps the parity test meaningful.

6.4 · Create ML, in one afternoon

For the image classifiers (R5, S2) the fastest honest path skips Python entirely. Export your stored receipts to folders named after their class, drop the parent folder into the Create ML app, choose Image Classification, press Train. Twenty minutes later you have a 2 MB Core ML model with a held-out accuracy report. 

# get the training set out of SQLite — one folder per class
cds-tsx run scripts/export-receipt-images.ts --out ~/twm-receipts
#   ~/twm-receipts/receipt/*.jpg
#   ~/twm-receipts/invoice/*.jpg

# then, to use it in lane B instead of lane D:

The catch worth knowing in advance: Create ML gives you Core ML, and Core ML does not run in a browser or on Linux. §7.2

Build the prompt from the row, never from a model

The temptation is to ask an LLM to write the image prompt. Don't — it adds a second source of invention on top of one that already invents. Compose it from fields: 

style   = "ink and watercolour wash, muted, no text, no faces"
subject = memory.place ?? event.place ?? category.name
season  = seasonOf(memory.occurredOn)
mood    = paletteFrom(dominantColours(memory.photos[0]))
prompt  = `${subject}, ${season}, ${mood}, ${style}`
seed    = hash(memory.ID)     // same memory, same picture, forever

Seeding from the row's own id is what makes the feature feel designed rather than random: re-running the job produces the same illustration, so nothing silently changes under somebody's memory.

§7.3

Provenance is not optional

Every generated image needs to be distinguishable from a photograph, in the database and on the screen. That is partly taste — this app is a record of real evenings — and partly the EU AI Act's transparency requirement for synthetic content. 

// db/schema.cds — additions to Photos / EventPhotos
generatedBy     : String(60);   // 'flux.1-schnell'; null = a real photo
generatedPrompt : LargeString;  // exactly what was asked for
generatedSeed   : Integer;
generatedAt     : Timestamp;

A non-null generatedBy draws the badge, excludes the image from the "hero photo" chooser in S3, and keeps it out of anything that claims to be a record of what happened.

§8

What not to build

A research document that only says yes is a sales brochure. These are the ideas that look attractive from here and are wrong, with the reason each one fails.

Fine-tuning an LLM on your ledger

A few hundred rows of transactions and forty memories is not a fine-tuning dataset; it is a prompt. You would spend a weekend to make a model marginally better at a task that retrieval plus a good system prompt already does better, and you would have to redo it every time the data grows. Retrieve, don't train.

A relationship score

The data would support it arithmetically and it would be a genuinely bad thing to exist. A number that goes down is a number people argue about, and no model here has the validity to make that claim. The app's own premise — nobody owes anybody — already refuses this kind of scoring; keep the refusal.

Inferring anything sensitive from purchases

Merchant strings leak health, religion, pregnancy and orientation with very little effort. ADR-002 §6 already refuses to store an orientation label; a model that infers one is the same decision made worse. Keep the category vocabulary closed, and never add a head whose classes you would not show the person.

Mood logged without a tap

Automatic mood capture — from the camera, from message sentiment, from typing speed — is technically easy and turns the app into something that watches. The existing design, where a model fills the picker and a person confirms, is right. It also happens to be what keeps the feature on the correct side of §9.

A multi-gigabyte model in the browser

WebGPU can run a 7B model in a tab. It will also download two gigabytes over a phone connection, fill the cache, and drain a battery. Keep lane B to models under about 100 MB — landmarks, embeddings, small classifiers — and put anything larger in lanes C or D.

Anything heavy inside the Fly image

The server is a small shared machine holding a SQLite file. An ONNX embedding model is fine; a diffusion pipeline, a transcription model or a second Python runtime are not. The rule of thumb: if it changes the image size by more than a couple of hundred megabytes, it belongs in lane C.

Replacing Document AI on a hunch

Vision's OCR is excellent and will be worse than Document AI on exactly the receipts that matter — faded thermal paper, folded, photographed at an angle. Run it beside the current path, compare on your stored archive, and only then decide which is the default. The raw extraction is kept verbatim precisely so this comparison is possible.

Learning across households

The moment the app holds more than one group, a model trained on everybody's corrections is a model that moved one household's data into another's predictions. Either keep base training to your own group's data, or aggregate in a way you can describe in one sentence to the people involved — and then actually describe it.

§9

The part that decides whether any of this is allowed

This app is Swiss-flavoured, holds data about identified people, infers something about their emotional state, and — since TWM-ADR-002 — is heading towards holding data about other people's households. That last change is the one that matters legally, and it is worth understanding before the mood model gets better rather than after.

The change nobody notices: the household exemption stops applying

Data protection law generally leaves purely personal or household activity alone. A two-person ledger you run for yourselves sits comfortably there. A multi-tenant service where other households register, invite each other and store their photos does not. At that point you are a controller, with the ordinary obligations that follow: a lawful basis, information duties, deletion, and a defensible answer to "what does the mood feature do with my face?"

None of that is a reason to stop. It is a reason that M1 — the on-device mood model — is the right architecture and not merely the cheap one: the strongest possible answer to that question is "the photograph never reaches us, and here is the code path that proves it".

Emotion inference, specifically

Inferring emotion from a face is not biometric identification, so the strictest category does not automatically apply — but an emotional state can reveal something about health, which is special-category data under both the GDPR and the revised Swiss FADP, and the EU AI Act treats emotion-recognition systems as a transparency-obligated class outside its outright bans (which cover workplaces and schools, not a household diary).

What that means concretely, and all of it is cheap: explicit opt-in per person, not per group; a plain sentence saying a model estimates the mood and can be wrong; the ability to delete every reading and every training sample; and no automatic logging. The code already satisfies most of this.

Generated images, specifically

Synthetic content carries a transparency expectation: a viewer should be able to tell that a picture was made by a model. In a private timeline this is mostly a design concern, but it becomes a real one the moment an image can be shared out of the app.

Do the simple version properly: the generatedBy provenance fields from §7.3, a visible badge on the image, and exclusion from anything that presents itself as a record. Embedded C2PA credentials are the thorough version if you ever add sharing.

The on-device ladder

A single rule that decides most of these questions for you, in order: compute it on the device; if not, compute it on your server; if not, send the minimum that answers the question; never send an image where a vector will do.

Every proposal in §4 was placed in its lane by walking down this ladder, not by asking what was easiest.

A model card per model

Half a page in docs/ for each shipped model: what it predicts, what it was trained on and how many rows, its measured accuracy on held-out data, its known failure modes, and the date. docs/MODEL.md is already exactly this for the classifier — copy its shape.

It costs an hour and it is the difference between a model you trust and a model you hope about.

Deletion has to reach the training set

If someone deletes their mood history, the blendshape samples that history produced must go too, and the next retrain must not contain them. That means training data is derived on demand from live tables — as ml:export-data already does — rather than accumulating in a file nobody remembers.

This section is an engineering summary, not legal advice. If the app ever takes on households outside your own, the mood feature and the photo storage are the two things worth twenty minutes of a lawyer's time.
